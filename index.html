Patrick Mutabazi

Authentication proves who logged in. I build security that proves what happens next: every high-impact action stays tied to the legitimate person and executes exactly what they approved.

My doctoral framework, Responsibility-Preserving Security, joins Zero Trust identity, ZT-TOTP, Actor Integrity (AIg) and Proof-of-Intent Authorization (PoIA) into one security chain, each link machine-checked with the Tamarin prover and tested in open prototypes. Alongside it, I apply machine learning to continuous authentication, and I am now extending this work to AI security. It is built for the systems millions of people in Sub-Saharan Africa rely on: mobile money, internet banking and digital public services.

About

I’m a doctoral researcher at the Cyber Resilience Laboratory, Nara Institute of Science and Technology (NAIST), Japan. My path started in Rwanda, where I studied and worked on national cyber security and digital government services. That is where I saw how much daily life now depends on digital systems being trustworthy after sign-in.

My work joins theory and practice. I state security properties precisely, prove them with the Tamarin prover, and build prototypes with evaluations designed so they can report failure. With collaborators, I also build and evaluate machine-learning models that confirm a user continuously from how they type, move and tap. Before my PhD I spent more than five years across industry and academia in security engineering, vulnerability assessment and penetration testing, including mapping and digitizing about 100 government service processes for Rwanda with GIZ and Irembo.

I care about growing the next generation of security talent. At Carnegie Mellon University Africa I was a graduate teaching assistant for Cyber Defense and Network Security and a volunteer mentor in CyLab-Africa outreach for girls and women in STEM. I also mentor emerging talent through the CyberTalents program, supported by Trend Micro.

Research

Modern systems check credentials, enforce access policies and assess device posture, yet failures still happen after access succeeds. Credentials survive a SIM swap, sessions persist after control changes, and authorized requests execute something the person never approved. My dissertation treats identity, actor continuity and intent as one chain, and secures each link. A complementary thread applies machine learning to continuous authentication in high-fraud environments.

  1. Legitimacy

    Is this the legitimate account holder?

    In much of Sub-Saharan Africa the phone number is the identity, so a swapped SIM can hand an attacker someone’s savings. My telecom-anchored Zero Trust IAM uses operator signals to catch SIM swap and recovery reassignment before they take over an account. ZT-TOTP hardens one-time passwords against phishing and relay attacks.

    SIM swap architecture, ICOIACT 2025. ZT-TOTP, IEEE CSR 2026.

  2. Actor continuity

    Is it still the same person in control?

    Passing a login says nothing about who acts next. Actor Integrity (AIg) preserves the link between an authenticated user and their later actions, using device, contextual and behavioural signals, and formalizes it so it can be verified.

    With collaborators, I apply machine learning to the same question: the KeystrokeMouse-TZ dataset, DABBiT for behavioural drift, an adaptation-governance framework published in IEEE Access, and CABA, which fuses behavioural and contextual signals to cut equal error rate by up to 65% within context and 71% across devices.

    AIg, under review at IJIS. Adaptation governance, IEEE Access 2026. CABA, ICCE-Asia 2026. DABBiT, AAIML 2026. KeystrokeMouse-TZ, APCC 2025.

  3. Intent integrity

    Is this exactly what they approved?

    Proof-of-Intent Authorization binds each protected execution to a single-use signed intent, with guarantees that run from commitment to execution (see PoIA below). Evaluated on banking and cloud-resource operations.

    PoIA, CSP 2026. Journal extension under review at IEEE Access.

Featured work, intent integrity

Proof-of-Intent Authorization (PoIA)

Enforcing intent integrity from commitment to execution

Malware does not need your password if it can act inside your session. PoIA lets a high-impact operation run only if it is exactly the one you approved.

A transaction generator steals nothing. It waits for a genuine login, then sends its own requests through the live session. Stronger authentication cannot see it, and trusted-device confirmation binds the signature only to what was displayed, not to what happens afterwards or to how that value was produced.

PoIA makes that binding an authorization condition. A protected operation executes only if a fresh, single-use proof over the relying party’s canonical intent is valid, and the executed request normalizes to the same semantics. The journal extension, now under review at IEEE Access, carries the guarantee across the whole lifecycle.

  1. Before signing

    k-of-n Independent Commitment Confinement

    At least k of n independent construction paths must agree on a value before it is shown and signed, so a single compromised source cannot decide what the person sees.

  2. At approval

    Intent Integrity

    The person signs a fresh, single-use proof over a canonical intent: the action, principal, scope, context and versioned referents. Execution must match it exactly.

  3. At execution

    Referent-State Integrity

    Referenced content, such as a stored beneficiary record, must still carry its committed version and digest, re-checked inside the transaction that performs the effect.

Together: Full-Lifecycle Non-Reconstitution. No execution can be assembled from an approval whose referent or commitment no longer holds.

Evidence

0 of 4,550
adversarial cases accepted by PoIA. The session-only and legacy baselines admitted every case they were paired on.
1,800 of 1,800
correctness cases classified correctly, scored by an independent checker against prespecified decisions.
6 Tamarin theories
five of them new. Removing either the signature check or the live-version check yields a counterexample.

to verify a signature locally, against multi-second human approval. Tested on two authenticator backends.
